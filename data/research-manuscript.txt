# Mining materials and power for the Super Intelligence buildout

Manufacturing servicing and industrial capacity in Australia and the world

Prepared for Luke Nathan Hayes on 7 November 2026

## Executive summary

The United States and China are driving an industrial expansion that Australia must meet with its own manufacturing, materials, power and defensive capability. These 7 November 2026 estimates compare projected need with current production and size an Australian robot industry for domestic use and export.

### Machines and materials

Accelerated robot case: 409.6 million newly manufactured during 2036; 1,052,537,500 manufactured cumulatively during 2027 to 2036. Operating fleet = accumulated manufacture minus permanent retirements. One billion remain operating if no more than 52.5 million of those machines leave service. That places this path around the scale of {musk-fleet|Musk's September 2026 prediction of more than a billion operating within ten years}. At 60 kilograms each, the 2031 batch contains 2.34 billion kilograms; the 2036 batch contains 24.6 billion. Repair and safer automated operation can preserve that equipment for longer service.

Copper: the January 2026 market outlook grows with power, transport and wider industry. Interpolating it gives 38.8 million tonnes of annual use in 2036. The accelerated robot batch adds 1.64 million to 3.28 million tonnes, taking end use to 40.4 million to 42.1 million, up from 28 million in 2025. Robot factory feed is 1.72 million to 3.45 million tonnes at 95 per cent yield. Half secondary feed leaves 862,000 tonnes to 1.72 million tonnes of primary copper, compared with Australia's 730,000 tonne annual mine output in 2025. {sp-demand|Dated market outlook}; {ga2026|Australian production}.

Aluminium: total annual use reaches 139.6 million to 142.9 million tonnes in the 2036 market extension plus robot case, against 106 million in the July 2025 outlook's starting year. Recycling and primary smelting grow separately. The robot primary addition is 3.66 million to 5.39 million tonnes, or six to nine Tomago-sized annual outputs. With the base-market gap filled through different primary and secondary mixes, world primary output needs 82.2 million to 88.1 million tonnes, against 74 million produced and 79.7 million of installed capacity in 2025. {al-demand|Market and supply outlook}; {aluminum|USGS benchmarks}.

Rare earth magnets: 2036 reference motors contain 246,000 to 369,000 tonnes of neodymium and praseodymium, or NdPr. Avoiding 90 per cent with other motors leaves 24,600 to 36,900 tonnes, four to six times Lynas's observed annual metal content of 6,220 tonnes through its Australia-Malaysia chain. Australia's planned Eneabba and Nolans refineries represent another roughly 7,000 tonnes annually as they ramp. {lynas|August 2026 operating output}; {iluka|Iluka November 2026}; {nolans|Nolans plans}.

### Power and the delivery pipeline

New clean supply must cover fossil replacement and essential growth alongside compute and robots. Any shortfall requires more generation. Ground computing used an estimated 485 terawatt-hours in 2025. The following scenarios assume 80 per cent average equipment load and 20 per cent facility overhead. Racks use a 1.47 tonne, 120 kilowatt reference. Australia generated 286.8 terawatt-hours in 2025. {iea|International Energy Agency April 2026 estimate}; {aus-power|Australian 2026 statistics}.

At 14.4 per cent annual electricity growth, 2036 consumption is 2,130 terawatt-hours, or 7.4 Australias. That year's net capacity addition is 32 gigawatts, housing 391,000 tonnes of additional racks.

At 20 per cent growth, 2036 consumption is 3,600 terawatt-hours, or 12.6 Australias. The annual addition is 71 gigawatts, housing 876,000 tonnes of additional racks.

At 30 per cent growth, 2036 consumption is 8,690 terawatt-hours, or 30.3 Australias. The annual addition is 239 gigawatts, housing 2.93 million tonnes of additional racks.

In October 2026, the Australian Energy Market Operator, AEMO, reported 80.9 gigawatts of generation and storage in the main grid's connection process, against 77 installed on that basis. The September quarter saw 4.5 gigawatts receive application approval and 1.3 reach full output. Berkeley Lab's June 2026 study recorded US applications for 1,312 gigawatts of generation and 749 of storage at the end of 2025; 549 had draft or executed connection agreements. Storage is separate from generation. {aemo-pipeline|AEMO October 2026}; {us-queue|Berkeley Lab June 2026}.

Globally, 2025 additions included about 511 gigawatts of solar, while the IEA's 2026 review reports 78 gigawatts of nuclear under construction. Supplying 100 gigawatts of rated computing from solar at a chosen 25 per cent capacity factor requires about 384 gigawatts of panels for annual energy, plus a design for delivery timing. With 65 per cent of energy stored and 90.25 per cent round-trip efficiency, that solar rating rises to about 411 gigawatts. A four-hour full-load buffer delivering 480 gigawatt-hours needs about 561 nominal at 90 per cent usable reserve and 95 per cent discharge efficiency, containing roughly 51,400 tonnes of cathode lithium. {irena2026|IRENA March 2026}; {nuclear2026|IEA nuclear construction}; {ga2026|updated mineral production}.

Orbital computing carries its power supply. The engineering ramp commissions 0.5, 5.5, 55, 250 and 300 gigawatts during 2027, 2028, 2029, 2031 and 2036. At an assumed 10,000 tonnes per gigawatt, the 2036 batch is three million tonnes and 30,000 payload flights at 100 tonnes delivered per flight. Better output per tonne reduces those requirements. {spacex|SpaceX June 2026 ambition}.

Australian manufacturing: the worked 2032 case produces 312,500 robots, including 109,375 exports, through eight factory modules. Factory and shared engineering capital totals A$1.04 billion; production working capital adds A$2.73 billion. By 2036, 39 modules support 1.687 million robots annually and about 3.059 million domestic machines in service. That year's construction and engineering account totals A$4.68 billion, plus A$14.76 billion of production cash. The full 2.048 million annual ambition needs 48 modules at the chosen throughput. These are 7 November scenarios in constant late-2025 Australian dollars, using a reported Texas Optimus floor area and separate Australian cost allowances. {au-manufacturing-page|Factory model and export opportunity}; {rlb-industrial-2026|building rates}; {tesla-observer-nov|5 November construction report}.

## The physical task

Super Intelligence, abbreviated here to SI, could accelerate the design and operation of computers, robots, energy systems and the factories that build them. This paper estimates the material requirements of that expansion over one, two, three, five and ten years from November 2026. It follows the physical chain from finished equipment through manufacturing, refining and source minerals, with comparisons to existing global and Australian industry.

The central opportunity is to build a growing stock of useful, repairable machines. New factories add robots and computing capacity. Workshops keep existing equipment working through worn-part replacement, refurbishment and processor upgrades. Better designs can increase the work delivered by each kilogram of material. Faster mining and refining can increase the material available to build the next generation.

Power is a major constraint on deployment. A chip factory can deliver processors before a suitable site has electricity, cooling and a connection ready for them. Governments and companies are responding with generation projects, storage, grid upgrades, nuclear development and new technologies. Space-based computing adds another route, carrying its power generation into orbit. Power beaming could connect separate generators and users. These developments change both the scale and the composition of material demand.

Power networks, transport, housing, water supply and ordinary industry expand alongside robots and computing. Their material requirements belong in the same plan. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, introduced below, reinforce the need to extend these services to more people. The calculations were rebuilt on 7 November 2026. They report the mass in manufactured equipment directly, then add specified service work and infrastructure. Source dates remain attached to reported production, applications, approvals and company targets. Future quantities are conditional estimates using the assumptions stated beside them.

## Manufactured equipment and the operating fleet

The accelerated manufacturing scenario produces about 39.1 million robots during 2031. At an assumed 60 kilograms each, those machines contain about 2.34 billion kilograms of finished equipment, or 2.34 million tonnes. That is the material in the batch manufactured that year. It remains a productive asset for as long as the machines and their components stay useful.

An Australian mass comparison helps picture it. Rio Tinto's {train|13 July 2018 report of its first autonomous Pilbara delivery} describes a train carrying about 28,000 tonnes of iron ore. The 2031 robot batch has the same mass as about 84 of those trainloads. This compares weight; robots occupy different space and require different transport arrangements.

A million kilograms is 1,000 tonnes. A billion kilograms is one million tonnes. Mass tells us the quantity of material. Volume also needs its density: a tonne of copper takes much less space than a tonne of aluminium. The metal-volume examples later in the paper supply both the density and the dimensions of the comparison.

Annual manufacturing and accumulated equipment answer different questions. Annual manufacturing determines that year's new material requirement. Accumulated manufacturing tells us how much equipment has been produced over several years. Equipment still in service is accumulated production less actual final retirements. A service job changes selected components within that base.

For 2031, the direct formula is 39,062,500 robots × 60 kilograms per robot = 2,343,750,000 kilograms of complete equipment.

For 2036, annual manufacturing is 409.6 million robots. Accumulated manufacturing from 2027 to 2036 is 1,052,537,500 robots. The operating fleet equals that accumulated production minus permanent retirements, plus any machines already operating before 2027. Counting the newly manufactured machines alone, one billion remain in use if no more than 52,537,500 have permanently left service.

Musk's {musk-fleet|1 September 2026 prediction} concerns more than one billion humanoid robots operating within ten years. The comparable figure in this model is the accumulated equipment still in service. Its 410 million annual factory output is a different measure. The manufacturing path is therefore around the scale of his fleet prediction, conditional on actual retention and repair.

## The material in one robot

Published designs vary. The {g1|Unitree G1 page checked on 6 November 2026} gives a mass of about 35 kilograms including its battery. {apollo|Apptronik's 2023 Apollo specifications} give 160 pounds, approximately 72.6 kilograms. The calculations use 60 kilograms as a clear whole-machine example within that product range.

CRU's {cru-materials|2025 component analysis}, checked on 6 November 2026, estimates 4 to 8 kilograms of copper and 17 to 25 kilograms of aluminium per humanoid robot. The following material calculations use those published ranges. A particular design still needs a measured inventory whose parts add up to its complete mass.

CRU's {cru|2026 magnet analysis}, checked on 5 November 2026, estimates 2 to 3 kilograms of neodymium-based magnets per humanoid. A 7 November assumption assigns 30 per cent of that alloy mass to neodymium and praseodymium combined, abbreviated NdPr. That gives 0.6 to 0.9 kilograms of NdPr per robot. The iron-magnet section tests how another motor design changes this requirement.

The {duke|Duke Humanoid V2 public bill of materials dated 19 September 2026} shows how to make the inventory auditable. It identifies 31 actuators, the computer, battery, cameras, machined components and other parts, with links between components and their place on the robot. Weighing those assemblies and obtaining supplier material declarations would connect each service job to its copper, aluminium, magnets and electronics.

Trace materials such as tin, tantalum, tungsten and silver need that same component evidence. Semiconductor production also needs high-purity inputs and specialised processing. Their requirement will follow the actual circuit boards, chips and manufacturing routes selected for each design.

Copper refining and aluminium smelting use the {usgs|US Geological Survey's 2026 compilation}, revised on 27 May 2026. These dated output figures show the scale of existing industry. Spare capacity requires a separate assessment of plants, feed, electricity and customer commitments.

Copper: world mines produced about 23 million tonnes of contained copper during 2025; Australian mines produced 730,000 tonnes. World refineries produced about 29 million tonnes, including metal from recycled feed.

Aluminium: world primary smelters produced about 74 million tonnes in 2025. Australia produced 1.57 million tonnes from four smelters, according to the Australian Aluminium Council. World installed annual smelter capacity was about 79.7 million tonnes.

Bauxite: world mine output was about 450 million tonnes in 2025; Australian output was 102.5 million tonnes. This is ore for the aluminium chain.

Iron: world mine output contained about 1.6 billion tonnes of iron in 2025; Australian output contained 596 million tonnes. Australia produced about 961 million tonnes of iron ore by bulk mass.

Lithium: the updated 2025 figures are about 314,000 tonnes worldwide and 118,000 tonnes in Australia, expressed as contained lithium. Geoscience Australia derives its Australian estimate from concentrate production and assumed lithium oxide content.

Graphite and rare earths: world natural graphite mine output was about 1.8 million tonnes in 2025; Australia recorded no production. Total rare earth oxide mine output was about 390,000 tonnes worldwide and 34,000 tonnes in Australia. NdPr is a specified portion of that mixed oxide output.

The {australian-al|Australian Aluminium Council's 2025 production figure}, checked on 7 November 2026, is 1.57 million tonnes from four smelters. Australia's bauxite mines, alumina refineries and metal smelters are successive stages. Large ore output gives Australia a strong starting position; additional finished-metal output needs processing and electricity as well.

USGS estimates world primary aluminium smelting capacity at about 79.7 million tonnes annually at the end of 2025, against actual output of about 74 million tonnes. The nominal difference is 5.7 million tonnes annually. Bringing unused capacity into production depends on its location, operating condition and electricity supply. New plant and recycled metal can add further output.

For a named Australian plant, {tomago|Tomago's company description checked on 7 November 2026} gives annual production of about 590,000 tonnes. Later comparisons use that figure so the reader can picture the requirement in terms of an existing Australian smelter.

## Five manufacturing horizons

The production scenarios were constructed on 5 November 2026 and recalculated on 7 November. The delayed path assumes slower deployment, the replicated path assumes factories expand through replication, and the accelerated path tests faster compound growth. The annual rates and starting points are recorded at the end of the paper. They are planning hypotheses.

Each horizon below states annual robot output, the complete mass of that year's accelerated batch at 60 kilograms per robot, and its estimated copper and aluminium. Material comparisons use the 2025 production benchmarks above. Service parts and factory losses are calculated separately.

### 2027

Annual manufacturing is 100,000 robots in the delayed path, 500,000 in the replicated path and 1 million in the accelerated path. At 60 kilograms each, the accelerated batch contains 60 million kilograms of equipment, or 60,000 tonnes.

That batch contains 4,000 to 8,000 tonnes of copper, equivalent to 0.0138 to 0.0276 per cent of world copper refining output in 2025. Its 17,000 to 25,000 tonnes of aluminium equal 1.08 to 1.59 per cent of Australia's 2025 primary aluminium output. These are finished materials before factory losses and secondary supply.

### 2028

Annual manufacturing is 150,000 robots in the delayed path, 1 million in the replicated path and 2.5 million in the accelerated path. At 60 kilograms each, the accelerated batch contains 150 million kilograms of equipment, or 150,000 tonnes.

That batch contains 10,000 to 20,000 tonnes of copper, equivalent to 0.0345 to 0.069 per cent of world copper refining output in 2025. Its 42,500 to 62,500 tonnes of aluminium equal 2.71 to 3.98 per cent of Australia's 2025 primary aluminium output. These are finished materials before factory losses and secondary supply.

### 2029

Annual manufacturing is 225,000 robots in the delayed path, 2 million in the replicated path and 6.25 million in the accelerated path. At 60 kilograms each, the accelerated batch contains 375 million kilograms of equipment, or 375,000 tonnes.

That batch contains 25,000 to 50,000 tonnes of copper, equivalent to 0.0862 to 0.172 per cent of world copper refining output in 2025. Annual manufacturing is 506,000 robots in the delayed path, 8 million in the replicated path and 39.1 million in the accelerated path. At 60 kilograms each, the accelerated batch contains 2.34 billion kilograms of equipment, or 2.34 million tonnes.

### 2036

Annual manufacturing is 1.54 million robots in the delayed path, 60.8 million in the replicated path and 410 million in the accelerated path. At 60 kilograms each, the accelerated batch contains 24.6 billion kilograms of equipment, or 24.6 million tonnes.

Its 6.96 million to 10.2 million tonnes of aluminium equal 4.44 to 6.52 times Australia's 2025 primary aluminium output. These are finished materials before factory losses and secondary supply.

Across 2027 to 2036, the accelerated path manufactures about 1.05 billion robots. At 60 kilograms each, that is approximately 63.2 billion kilograms, or 63.2 million tonnes, of complete equipment produced over the decade. The number still working in 2036 depends on their actual service histories. Useful old machines continue contributing to that stock.

## Required feed and additional industrial capacity

Growing power systems, transport, construction and industry form the demand base for the robot expansion. The following 7 November calculations use published market outlooks, then identify the additional accelerated robot batch of 409.6 million machines in 2036. Every quantity below is an annual flow.

### Development creates demand across the economy

The {sdg-agenda|United Nations 2030 Agenda, adopted on 25 September 2015}, sets Sustainable Development Goals for water and sanitation, energy access, industry and infrastructure, sustainable communities and resource efficiency. These are commonly called the UN SDGs. Meeting them involves pumps, pipes, power lines, generation, transport, buildings and equipment. The {sdg-2026|UN's 7 July 2026 progress report} calls for greater investment and a faster energy transition. Project funding and delivery determine the quantities built.

Think of a new Australian suburb. It needs houses, water, sewerage, roads, transformers and communications together. A robot factory is one more customer for the metals used in those systems. The same applies when a town in another country gains reliable electricity or water for the first time. These needs grow alongside the industrial expansion examined here.

### Copper demand and factory feed

S and P Global's {sp-demand|8 January 2026 copper study} projects total annual use of about 28 million tonnes in 2025, 33 million in 2030, 38 million in 2035 and 42 million in 2040. Its main total includes economic activity, energy systems, AI and data centres, and defence; humanoid manufacturing is discussed separately. A straight interpolation between its 2035 and 2040 figures gives a 2036 market base of 38.8 million tonnes. That interpolation is this paper's 7 November calculation.

409.6 million robots × 4 to 8 kilograms of copper each = 1.64 million to 3.28 million tonnes in finished robots. Adding that batch gives total annual end use of 40.4 million to 42.1 million tonnes in 2036, compared with 28 million in the study's 2025 demand base. The increase is 12.4 million to 14.1 million tonnes, or 44 to 50 per cent over eleven years. Meeting this end use requires about 3.40 to 3.77 per cent annual growth in metal delivered to customers. It also requires replacement capacity for any existing production that declines.

For the extra robot factories, 95 per cent net factory yield means 1.72 million to 3.45 million tonnes of external annual feed. Half qualified secondary input leaves 862,000 tonnes to 1.72 million tonnes of new primary copper. These are procurement quantities; the preceding market total measures metal in end uses. World mines produced about 23 million tonnes of contained copper in 2025 and refineries produced 29 million tonnes including recycled feed. Refinery output, end use and mine output have different boundaries. {copper|USGS 2026 production estimates}.

The half-secondary robot case needs reference ore throughput of 203 million to 406 million tonnes annually, using 0.5 per cent copper grade and 85 per cent combined recovery. Its primary metal equals 1.18 to 2.36 years of Australia's 730,000 tonne 2025 mine output, delivered within one year. Its ore mass is equivalent to 21 to 42 per cent of Australia's 961 million tonne iron ore output. The {ga2026|12 October 2026 Australian production tables} provide those benchmarks. Copper needs its own processing route.

With zero secondary input, the robot case needs 1.72 million to 3.45 million tonnes of primary copper and 406 million to 812 million tonnes of reference ore. At 25 per cent secondary input it needs 1.29 million to 2.59 million tonnes of primary metal and 304 million to 609 million tonnes of ore. Secondary supply needs collection, processing and an allocation to these customers.

### Aluminium demand and processing capacity

McKinsey's {al-demand|24 July 2025 aluminium outlook} projects total annual demand rising from 106 million tonnes in 2025 to 130 million in 2035, including growing energy and transport uses. Its separate 2035 supply outlook is 78 million tonnes of primary metal plus 48 million tonnes of secondary metal: 126 million tonnes in total, leaving a four million tonne market gap. These quantities separate new smelting from recycling.

Extending each of those published series by its own compound rate for one further year gives a 2036 demand base of 132.7 million tonnes, primary supply of 78.5 million and secondary supply of 50.0 million. The remaining base-market gap is 4.18 million tonnes. This one-year extension is a 7 November assumption. Treating the modelled humanoid batch as additional adds 6.96 million to 10.2 million tonnes, taking total end use to 139.6 million to 142.9 million tonnes in 2036. That is 33.6 million to 36.9 million tonnes above 2025 use, or 32 to 35 per cent higher. The published outlook does not identify a humanoid allowance; any such volume must be deducted before adding the batch.

The robot factories require 7.33 million to 10.8 million tonnes of annual feed at 95 per cent net yield. With half secondary feed, they require 3.66 million to 5.39 million tonnes each of primary and secondary metal. Australia produced 1.57 million tonnes of primary aluminium in 2025. The robot primary addition alone equals 2.33 to 3.43 years of that output, or 6.2 to 9.1 operations at Tomago's stated 590,000 tonne annual scale. {australian-al|Australian 2025 output}; {tomago|Tomago scale checked 7 November 2026}.

Adding robot primary feed to the extended supply pipeline requires 82.2 million to 83.9 million tonnes of annual primary output, alongside its new secondary feed. The wider market still needs the 4.18 million tonne gap filled. Filling it entirely through secondary supply requires 57.8 million to 59.6 million tonnes of secondary output and keeps primary output at the lower range. Filling it entirely through primary metal requires 86.4 million to 88.1 million tonnes of primary output and 53.7 million to 55.4 million tonnes of secondary output. Intermediate mixes sit between those cases.

Across those supply choices, primary output of 82.2 million to 88.1 million tonnes is 8.18 million to 14.1 million tonnes above the USGS 2025 output estimate of 74 million. That is roughly 14 to 24 additional Tomago-sized annual outputs globally. It also exceeds the USGS end-2025 installed smelter capacity of 79.7 million tonnes by 2.48 million to 8.38 million tonnes. The older market outlook used 73 million tonnes for 2025 primary supply; the comparison here retains the newer USGS 74 million tonne production benchmark. {aluminum|USGS 2026 aluminium estimates}.

For robot feed alone, zero secondary input requires 7.33 million to 10.8 million tonnes of primary aluminium and 29.3 million to 43.1 million tonnes of reference bauxite. At 25 per cent secondary input it requires 5.50 million to 8.08 million tonnes of primary metal and 22.0 million to 32.3 million tonnes of bauxite. At 50 per cent it requires 3.66 million to 5.39 million tonnes of primary metal and 14.7 million to 21.6 million tonnes of bauxite. The last ore quantity equals 14 to 21 per cent of Australia's 102.5 million tonne 2025 output. The process assumption is four tonnes of dry bauxite per tonne of primary metal.

The market bases already include substantial power, transport and construction demand. ## Put the metal volumes in familiar dimensions

For the accelerated 2036 batch, the copper estimate is about 1.64 million to 3.28 million tonnes. At {density-cu|Geoscience Australia's copper density of 8.96 tonnes per cubic metre}, this occupies about 183,000 to 366,000 cubic metres as solid metal. Picture a rectangular 50 metre swimming pool, 25 metres wide and two metres deep: 2,500 cubic metres. The copper would fill approximately 73 to 146 of those spaces. This calculation is dated 7 November 2026.

The aluminium estimate for that same year's robots is 6.96 million to 10.2 million tonnes. At {density-al|Geoscience Australia's aluminium density of 2.7 tonnes per cubic metre}, it occupies approximately 2.58 million to 3.79 million cubic metres as solid metal, enough for about 1,030 to 1,520 of the defined pool spaces. At roughly 410 million robots annually, reducing a material allowance by one kilogram per robot saves about 410,000 tonnes in that year's production. This 7 November calculation explains why modest improvements in each joint, frame or enclosure have industrial consequences at scale.

## Equipment life with repair and safer automation

Human-operated cars provide an observed starting point for repairable equipment. Their survival records include accident losses, driving behaviour and decisions about whether to repair. Australia already supports long lives through mechanics, spare parts, wreckers, rebuilders and secondhand sales. The {ntc|National Transport Commission's report using January 2025 registrations}, checked on 6 November 2026, found that about half of Australian light vehicles remain in use for around 19 years. This is a survival estimate: the age at which about half have left service.

The US evidence shows improvement. Estimates for 2003 and 2020 reproduced in {cars|Greene and Leard's March 2023 research review} put median passenger-car survival at 14.9 and 16.1 years. For SUVs and vans it increased from 16.6 to 18.2 years, and for pickups from 18.7 to 24 years. Manufacturing quality, maintenance, repair economics and owner decisions contribute to those outcomes.

The {ukcars|UK research reported on 24 January 2025} estimated median lives of 18.7 years for petrol cars, 16.8 for diesel cars and 18.4 for battery electric vehicles, using roadworthiness records from 2005 to 2022. These statistical estimates show how newer equipment can mature towards long service lives.

A robot's operating hours and loads will matter alongside age. A household robot used intermittently faces a different wear pattern from one working continuous factory shifts. Batteries, bearings, seals, motors, cameras and computers have different maintenance needs. A sound frame can support several generations of components, much as a well-maintained ute can keep working through changes of tyres, gearbox and engine.

Automation can improve that starting point. Precise motion, smoother acceleration, collision avoidance and early fault detection could reduce damage and preserve frames and components. In its {waymo-safety|24 September 2026 update}, Waymo reported 82 per cent fewer injury-causing crashes and 95 per cent fewer serious-injury crashes than local human benchmarks, across more than 270 million autonomous miles through June in five US service areas. Those results support testing substantial safety gains. Machine longevity needs the rate of permanent equipment loss, which is a different measurement from injuries.

The long-life case therefore separates permanent accident losses from fatigue or corrosion that cannot be repaired, and from owner decisions to retire equipment. A damaged panel, worn bearing or upgraded processor enters the service-parts account while the machine remains in use. Precise operation and predictive maintenance can reduce those repair needs as well. Continuous work increases exposure to wear, so service records need operating hours and loads alongside calendar age.

For a transparent 7 November accident sensitivity, start with 1,000 machines that would otherwise be permanently lost in destructive accidents. A 90 per cent reduction leaves 100 lost and retains 900. A near-zero-loss case of 99 per cent reduction leaves ten lost and retains 990. At 60 kilograms each, those cases preserve 54,000 and 59,400 kilograms of complete machines respectively, before any repairs. The percentages are scenario inputs. Waymo's injury figures are evidence for the direction of improvement, rather than a measured conversion to robot losses.

The fleet calculation applies final retirements to each year's surviving batch. With the accelerated manufacturing path, annual final-retirement sensitivities of 0.25, 0.5 and one per cent leave approximately 1.049 billion, 1.045 billion and 1.038 billion robots operating at the end of 2036. These are all-cause survival cases, calculated on 7 November using end-year batches. Lower destructive-accident rates improve survival within them. Much of this rapidly expanding fleet is young, so these ten-year results do not establish a full lifetime.

Twenty to thirty years of supported service is a useful design hypothesis for repairable machines. It requires durable retained structures, available parts and continued software support. The model carries useful equipment forward and adds actual service parts and final losses. Human-driven car survival supplies evidence of what a mature repair economy already achieves; automation provides a reason to test improvements beyond it.

## Servicing keeps material productive

Industrial robotics already has repair and remanufacturing businesses. In its {fanuc|service report checked on 6 November 2026}, FANUC describes about 80,000 repairs annually in Japan, including support for industrial products manufactured more than 40 years earlier. ABB's {abb|robot remanufacturing procedure checked on the same date} includes a 152-point inspection, replacement of worn or failed parts, reassembly and at least 16 hours of load testing.

Computing has a similar reuse chain. Microsoft reported {microsoft-reuse|on 17 April 2025} that it reused 3.2 million components during 2024. Processors, memory and other assemblies can move into further service after their first installation. Robot manufacturers also provide accessible modules: the {h1|Unitree H1 specifications checked on 6 November 2026} describe a quickly replaceable battery, while {apollo|Apptronik's 2023 Apollo announcement} describes swappable batteries.

A wider robot service industry could include independent workshops, mobile technicians, joint rebuilding, battery refurbishment, sensor calibration, processor upgrades and certified used parts. Access to service information and compatible components would help it grow. Continuing software support and owner-controlled backups would preserve useful machines through changes of supplier.

People may also become attached to a familiar robot's voice, movement, memories and behaviour. This is a 7 November 2026 ownership hypothesis: that attachment could support spending on refurbishment and continuity of identity. It strengthens the commercial case for repairable designs, although service-life estimates still need observed operating histories.

For a concrete service calculation, assume a 60 kilogram robot receives an 18 kilogram overhaul kit: a 12 kilogram battery module, a two kilogram controller assembly and four kilograms of worn mechanical parts. It retains 42 kilograms of serviceable equipment. These component masses are engineering assumptions dated 7 November, to be replaced by a measured service specification.

One million such service jobs require 18 million kilograms of modules, or 18,000 tonnes, while retaining 42 million kilograms of existing equipment. If half the four kilogram mechanical allowance can be reconditioned, the newly supplied material becomes 16 million kilograms, or 16,000 tonnes, for those same million jobs. The required workshop capacity follows the number of service jobs actually scheduled.

For an individual machine, an initial 60 kilograms plus three 18 kilogram overhauls means 114 kilograms of finished equipment and parts supplied over its service history. If that history spans twenty years, the frame and retained components support twenty years of use. The calculation sizes a repairable design; its actual durability must be demonstrated in operation.

## Ground computing needs a range of power expansion cases

These electricity paths describe requirements to be supplied. A valid supply plan also meets clean-energy transition targets, essential services and wider development. New clean generation is first allocated to replacing fossil electricity, serving essential demand growth and replacing retired clean supply. The remaining delivered energy can support additional compute and robots. Any shortfall stays visible as additional generation and network capacity to build. These are planning requirements adopted on 7 November 2026.

The {iea|IEA's 16 April 2026 computing-energy outlook} estimated 485 terawatt-hours of worldwide data-centre electricity in 2025 and projected 950 in 2030. That implies about 14.4 per cent compound annual growth between those dates. This paper tests three ground-computing paths from the 2025 starting point: continuing that 14.4 per cent rate through 2036, 20 per cent annual growth, and 30 per cent annual growth. All are 7 November 2026 scenarios.

Under those inputs, one gigawatt of rated computing consumes about 8.41 terawatt-hours annually, including overhead.

## Generation and storage also require materials

The power supply belongs in the industrial material account. Consider 100 gigawatts of rated computing equipment at 80 per cent average load and a facility power factor of 1.2. Average facility load is 96 gigawatts, consuming about 841 terawatt-hours annually, or 2.93 times Australia's 2025 generation. This is a 7 November design example.

At a 25 per cent solar capacity factor, annual energy before storage losses requires 384 gigawatts of panels. If 65 per cent of delivered electricity passes through batteries with 95 per cent charging and 95 per cent discharging efficiency, the required panel rating rises to about 411 gigawatts. That is about 80 per cent of the world's 2025 solar additions. The formula is 96 gigawatts × [0.35 + 0.65 divided by 0.9025] divided by 0.25. All operating shares and efficiencies are explicit 7 November assumptions.

With 23 per cent cell efficiency and 120 micrometres of active silicon, that 411 gigawatt installation contains approximately 500,000 tonnes of active silicon. At ten milligrams of silver per nameplate watt it needs about 4,110 tonnes of silver. These describe a selected panel recipe. Thinner wafers, alternative absorber materials and lower-silver or copper contacts change the inventory. Glass, frames, inverters, supports and networks must be added from the actual project design.

A four-hour buffer at the full 120 gigawatt facility load must deliver 480 gigawatt-hours. With 90 per cent usable state of charge and 95 per cent discharge efficiency, nominal stored energy must be about 561 gigawatt-hours: 480 divided by 0.90 and 0.95. Using the lithium iron phosphate cathode recipe specified later, that requires about 51,400 tonnes of cathode lithium. It equals 16 per cent of world 2025 lithium mine output and 44 per cent of Australian output on {ga2026|Geoscience Australia's updated basis}.

A sixteen-hour buffer at the same full facility load must deliver 1,920 gigawatt-hours and needs about 2,250 gigawatt-hours nominal. The selected cathode contains approximately 206,000 tonnes of lithium, equal to 66 per cent of world 2025 mine output or 1.75 years of Australian output. These battery quantities are manufactured stocks for the installation. Complete packs add anodes, electrolyte, current collectors, enclosures and controls. Different chemistries change the elemental requirement.

Annual energy and buffer duration are separate tests. Seasonal weather, low-output spells, charging power, networks and outages determine dependable supply. Nuclear, wind, hydro, solar, storage and shared networks provide different combinations. Fusion adds a further firm-power route if projects demonstrate reliable net electricity and repeatable construction. Every pathway needs a material inventory and commissioning schedule. Power allocated to clean-energy transition and essential development remains protected while this new load is supplied.

An illustrative one gigawatt orbital design needs about 4.25 square kilometres of active solar cells at 23 per cent efficiency, a 0.90 output factor and a 20 per cent power allowance, using the {solar|solar irradiance reference checked on 5 November 2026}. That active area is a square about 2.06 kilometres on each side. The design assumes continuous illumination; the selected orbit determines eclipse periods, storage and redundancy.

At 90 micrometres of active silicon, those cells contain about 892 tonnes of silicon. Packaging, wiring and supports add mass. These 7 November calculations help test whether the complete spacecraft mass budget can support its generation and cooling.

A germanium substrate produces a different mineral requirement from a thin silicon design. Computing performance, solar efficiency, radiator temperature, repair access and launch capacity can all change the mass per useful gigawatt.

## Power beaming adds connections between generators and users

Small-scale wireless power transmission has been demonstrated. {japan-beam|Japan Space Systems' report of 13 December 2024} describes aircraft-to-ground transmission over more than five kilometres, with beam control at 5.8 gigahertz. This demonstrates part of the control and transmission chain needed for a future space solar system.

Australia's {aquila|Aquila project record checked on 7 November 2026} reports laboratory tests transferring 200 watts of optical power to a moving receiver and 300 watts to a stationary receiver. Its Earth-to-orbit Bifrost work is listed as a planned development. These are company-reported optical power tests; delivered electrical output needs its own conversion measurement.

Caltech's {caltech-beam|1 June 2023 report on its MAPLE experiment} describes wireless power transfer in orbit and detection on Earth of a beam directed from the spacecraft. Together, these demonstrations establish working transmission and control hardware at early scales. Larger applications need measured power delivered, efficiency, pointing performance and operating availability.

The possible uses include supplying other satellites, extending spacecraft service, powering moving machines and delivering orbital solar electricity to receivers on Earth. Those uses connect directly to the repair economy: an external energy supply could preserve a spacecraft or robot whose original power system limits its useful service.

For a direct 7 November sizing example, delivering one gigawatt of electricity through a beam requires two gigawatts from the source at 50 per cent end-to-end electrical efficiency. At 80 per cent efficiency it needs 1.25 gigawatts; at 25 per cent it needs four. These are engineering sensitivities, not measured efficiencies for the demonstrations above. They show how the selected link changes generator, transmitter, receiver and heat-rejection requirements.

An orbital computer using electricity locally and a satellite delivering electricity to Earth need different hardware inventories. Each generator's output can be allocated among its users, with losses counted along the chosen route. That prevents the same solar electricity being assigned simultaneously to a full computing load and a full export load.

## From manufactured material to source minerals

A mine supplies ore or concentrate. Refineries produce useful chemicals or metals. Component factories turn that material into parts. The calculation starts with the finished material, allows for net manufacturing losses, subtracts qualified recycled supply and then works backwards through the selected mineral process.

Atomic composition fixes the chemical conversions. Using the {atomic|2024 atomic-weight table checked on 5 November 2026}, pure hematite contains about 69.9 per cent iron and magnetite about 72.4 per cent. Pure chalcopyrite contains approximately 34.6 per cent copper. An actual ore deposit mixes these minerals with other rock, so its measured grade determines how much must be processed.

For a copper ore grading 0.5 per cent copper, 200 tonnes of ore contain one tonne of copper before losses. At 85 per cent combined recovery to the required metal, approximately 235 tonnes of ore deliver one tonne. These grade and recovery assumptions are a 7 November reference process. A specific mine's assays and recovery results provide the project calculation.

Aluminium oxide contains about 52.9 per cent aluminium. Bauxite is a mixed ore whose grade and processing performance determine how much is required. The capacity calculation uses four tonnes of dry bauxite per tonne of primary aluminium as a stated engineering assumption.

Lithium carbonate contains about 18.8 per cent lithium, so one tonne of lithium corresponds to approximately 5.32 tonnes of lithium carbonate. A project reporting lithium carbonate equivalent must be converted before comparison with an elemental-lithium requirement. For a hard-rock ore grading one per cent lithium oxide, with 75 per cent overall recovery, approximately 287 tonnes of ore deliver one tonne of lithium under the 7 November reference process.

Monazite is a family of rare earth phosphate minerals. The mix of neodymium, praseodymium and other elements varies by deposit. Mineral-sands stockpiles can provide feed for refining; the relevant quantities are their actual mineral content and separation performance. A refinery's output is usefully specified by individual oxide or metal, rather than only a total rare earth basket.

For a rare earth ore grading five per cent total oxides, assume NdPr oxides form 20 per cent of those oxides, their contained-metal factor is 85.7 per cent, and overall recovery is 75 per cent. One tonne of that ore then delivers about 6.43 kilograms of NdPr metal; one tonne of NdPr needs about 156 tonnes of ore. These 7 November assumptions describe an example route to be replaced with the selected deposit and refinery data.

For the battery example above, lithium iron phosphate active cathode delivering 150 ampere-hours per kilogram at 3.2 volts needs about 2.08 kilograms per kilowatt-hour. Its lithium content is approximately 0.092 kilograms per kilowatt-hour, with 0.737 kilograms of iron and 0.409 of phosphorus. These 7 November design values cover the active cathode. A complete battery inventory adds the other materials and uses the chemistry actually selected.

## Improvements can compound alongside production

Manufacturing and materials efficiency can improve together. If annual output doubles while material per unit falls by 20 per cent, total material demand rises by 60 per cent. If material per unit halves, doubled output uses the same total material. These 7 November relationships make the effect of better designs measurable.

The mine can also improve. Raising recovery from 85 to 95 per cent reduces ore required for a fixed output by about 11 per cent. Better sorting can improve the grade entering the processing plant, while new extraction routes can make additional feed useful. Actual material handling and process performance determine the gain.

Google DeepMind's {gnome|GNoME announcement of 29 November 2023} describes a large expansion in predicted stable crystal structures. The {alab|autonomous synthesis research published that day} demonstrates progress towards preparing selected materials. A useful industrial advance then needs repeatable performance, manufacturing yield and a route to volume production.

There are operating-industry examples of computational improvement. {bhpai|BHP and Microsoft's 30 May 2023 announcement} describes AI-assisted copper recovery at Escondida. Rio Tinto's {nuton|15 January 2026 Nuton announcement} links copper-processing development with Amazon Web Services. Future SI could accelerate these feedbacks through better design, control and experimentation.

Atomically precise manufacturing could further change structure, waste and component performance. Its contribution can enter the model as demonstrated material savings, higher accepted output or longer service. The growth scenarios remain open to those improvements and to faster factory replication. Measured results determine how strongly the feedback compounds.

Grid-delivered consumption rises from 175.7 to 249.4 terawatt-hours, another 73.7. These figures cover the eastern and southern interconnected market; Western Australia, the Northern Territory and other isolated systems require their own plans.

Population growth alone therefore understates the industrial opportunity. The published demand path includes electrification and emerging industry. Australia can supply switchboards, transformers, cables, towers, solar mounting, power electronics, batteries, water systems and the machines that install and service them. The 80.9 gigawatts in AEMO's October 2026 connection process, recorded in the applications section, provides a dated list of prospective customers to examine. ### Start with useful work and existing Australian capability

Australia already builds robots. The Clean Energy Finance Corporation's {swarmfarm-cefc|October 2025 case study} records A$7 million committed to SwarmFarm, a Toowoomba manufacturing facility and about 200 robots then deployed in Australia. The Australian Renewable Energy Agency's {luminous-arena-2026|project commencing 12 October 2026} supports Luminous Robotics establishing Melbourne manufacturing and scaling robots for utility-scale solar construction. These are foundations for further capacity.

The {robotics-strategy|May 2024 National Robotics Strategy} reported about A$18 billion of Australian robotics-company revenue in 2021. That historical figure covers a broad industry, rather than a measured humanoid production line. Queensland's {qld-manufacturing-hubs|manufacturing hub network, checked 7 November 2026}, provides regional connections for firms that need fabrication, automation and supplier capability.

Initial civic orders could cover solar construction and maintenance, farm work, inspection, moving materials, water and stormwater maintenance, public-space care and emergency logistics. Select machines against demonstrated tasks. Wheeled platforms, industrial arms and specialist field robots can enter service alongside humanoid designs. Specify repair manuals, replaceable parts, local operating authority, interoperable interfaces and continuing access to the machine's data and controls when buying the equipment.

### Tesla is already building the production system

Tesla's {tesla-robot-q1-2026|22 April 2026 update} described a Fremont Optimus line designed for one million robots annually and a Texas second-generation line designed for a long-term ten million annually. Its {tesla-robot-q3-2026|21 October 2026 update} reports production starting on Fremont's high-volume lines, classifies California as early ramp and Texas as construction, and leaves the Optimus capacity cells blank. The million and ten-million figures remain design ambitions. The October filing supplies a newer construction and production status.

That floor area describes the broader vehicle manufacturing facility. The Optimus portion of Fremont's converted Model S and Model X space is not separately sized in the reviewed updates.

In a {tesla-observer-nov|5 November 2026 construction report}, drone observer Joe Tegtmeyer describes the new Texas Optimus building as exceeding seven million square feet, with substantial sections using four floors. Seven million square feet converts to about 650,000 square metres, or 65 hectares of total floor space across its levels. This is a reported construction-area benchmark, not a Tesla-certified floor schedule. The source is the observer's own account reproduced by an X web viewer. The calculator makes the area editable. The same observer expected limited production late in 2027 and a larger ramp through 2028; Tesla's October update supplies no completion date for the Texas factory.

Those quantities show the scale of the industrial race. The reviewed Tesla disclosures do not itemise an Optimus-only capital budget. ### Translate the Tesla benchmark into a testable local hypothesis

Ten million robots divided by the reported 650,000 square metres gives roughly 15.4 robots per square metre per year at Tesla's intended mature scale. The worked Australian case uses one quarter of that throughput per productive square metre, then applies a 75 per cent achieved-output factor. The 20,000 square metre module has 15,000 productive square metres. Its resulting annual output ceiling is about 43,250 robots. This is a 7 November 2026 engineering hypothesis, not observed Australian or Tesla production performance.

The calculator lets readers change the reference floor area, reference production target, Australian throughput, achieved output and support space. It then limits annual manufacture to the factories scheduled to be available. A larger sales or production ambition appears as an unmet factory requirement until the physical capacity is added. Components, skills, power, validated designs and committed buyers must also support the production schedule.

### Domestic use and exports grow together

Permanent fleet losses use the paper's repair-preserving assumptions. Every figure in this programme is a conditional 7 November 2026 scenario.

In 2032, eight modules provide 160,000 square metres of enclosed floor. They support the scenario's 312,500 robots manufactured that year: 203,125 for domestic use and 109,375 for export. Earlier domestic batches leave about 412,000 machines in service by that year. The construction and shared engineering account totals A$1.04 billion. Three months of working capital at A$35,000 variable production cost per robot requires another A$2.73 billion. Buildings and production cash are separate funding needs.

In 2036, the same factory ramp reaches 39 modules and 780,000 square metres of total floor space. It supports about 1.687 million robots manufactured that year, against a 2.048 million ambition. Meeting the full ambition at the selected throughput requires 48 modules, nine more than scheduled. The supported batch supplies about 1.096 million domestic robots and 590,000 exports. Increasing the factory rollout multiplier from 1.50 to 1.54 reaches 49 modules by 2036 and covers the 2.048 million annual production ambition under the same productivity assumptions. ### Make the missing middle a durable domestic customer

At 3,000 locally governed service catchments, the slower worked path's 2032 domestic fleet averages about 137 robots per catchment; its 2036 fleet averages about 1,020. Those machines can work across surrounding homes, farms, utilities and public spaces. They need local workshops, trained crews, parts inventories, software continuity and independent repair businesses. The racks and workshops support their work; they do not place the entire fleet under one remote command.

At 250 watts average wall power per operating robot, the 2036 domestic fleet needs about 6.70 terawatt-hours annually. Factory assembly and testing add about 0.84 terawatt-hours at an assumed 500 kilowatt-hours per machine produced. Upstream metals, chips and component manufacture require their own inventories. New generation, storage and network connections must meet this programme alongside fossil replacement and essential development. Robots that help build and maintain clean energy are part of the delivery capacity.

Begin with domestic service contracts and qualified production batches. Grow Australian manufacture of frames, wiring, control boards, battery packs, joints, sensors, tools and repair modules as those processes are proven. Secure diverse imported components while expanding local production and recoverable inventories. Procurement can preserve owner-held keys, local data, documented interfaces, physical isolation and the ability to disconnect.

Export machines, modules and service knowledge on terms that preserve the buyer's authority. GAJRA Earth, the Global Association for Joyful Responsible Abundance on Earth, provides Hayes's proposed international setting for that reciprocal capability. The aim is a growing network of capable communities and countries. Australia's opportunity is to become a manufacturer, designer, repairer and exporter with its own domestic productive base as the global industry grows.

## Sovereignty and the global missing middle

The United States and China are the principal industrial forces in this analysis. China's national programmes and the United States' government-backed and corporate programmes mobilise energy, chip production and manufacturing at a scale that affects other countries' choices. The {china-power|23 July 2026 Chinese energy plan} and the {us-emergency|20 April 2026 US infrastructure determination} illustrate the state role. Large founder-led business networks also mobilise corporate spending, investor finance and supply commitments across connected industries.

Australia enters this competition with substantial material leverage. The {ga2026|October 2026 Geoscience Australia tables} place its 2025 shares at about 37 per cent of world iron ore output, 23 per cent of bauxite and 38 per cent of lithium. Its roles differ by material and processing stage. The capacity comparisons above show where additional domestic refining and manufacture could strengthen its position.

The objective proposed here is Australian authority over its resources and supply decisions, including the ability to negotiate between major customers and withstand coercion from any country. It includes physical defence: protecting mines, processing plants, ports, power systems, communications and transport against attack or seizure, while maintaining essential production and services during disruption.

Supply agreements can support that authority through diverse customers, domestic processing, reliable civilian access and investment in Australian skills and infrastructure. A defence and industrial strategy should address attempted coercion by the United States, China or any other state on the same principle of Australian self-determination.

Hayes's proposed defence is a distributed missing middle: civic robots, workshops, manufacturing capability, power and shared computing owned and governed across people and communities. A single organisation does not command the whole network. Civic machines can maintain infrastructure, move supplies, support food and health services and repair the systems on which national continuity depends. Owners and communities retain operating authority while coordinating agreed work.

{fairgo|A Fair Go in the Age of Superintelligence}, checked on 5 November 2026, connects national capability with postcode-level sovereign compute. Its racks are physical infrastructure. Aura of Intelligence is Hayes's proposed personal software. Each individual Aura can use that shared capacity under its owner's authority. The Web3 sensorium is the proposed intermediary layer; {p4a|Purple Party for Australia, or P4A}, is the proposed civic layer for informed grassroots participation and constitutional change.

Domestic component manufacture, repair workshops and shared computing help people retain useful capability. Owners need compatible parts, control of records and the ability to connect or disconnect. The same industrial base can support hospitals, science and civilian infrastructure or military equipment. Public investment can secure transparent supply commitments and accountable control as production expands.

The same missing middle is intended to grow globally. {gajra|GAJRA Earth, the Global Association for Joyful Responsible Abundance on Earth}, provides the public framework for that wider proposal. Communities in many countries would build and govern their own useful capacity while sharing knowledge and coordinating chosen projects. Hayes describes this as the world balancing itself through distributed capability. Australia is one participant in that network, and its national self-defence sits within a wider aim of personal and community sovereignty.

In that proposal, joyful, responsible and abundant are held together: people have worthwhile lives, take responsibility for their effects on others and expand the capacity to meet needs. The quantitative question remains concrete. How many civic machines, workshops, power systems and processing plants can communities actually obtain, maintain and govern? The material and capacity comparisons in this paper identify the industrial scale from which to plan that distributed buildout.

Hayes also expresses this purpose through {protopian|A Protopian Gambit} and its songs We Go Beyond and Joyful Responsible Abundance. The invitation is to build a world people want to participate in, with room for exploration, enjoyment and responsibility towards others. Civic robots, personal Auras and shared infrastructure serve that life. The three values guide choices about what machines do, who gains access and who is affected.

## Calculation record

All calculations in this rewrite are dated 7 November 2026. The robot production paths originated on 5 November. Reported observations and targets retain their source dates. Narrative quantities are rounded from unrounded calculations.

The delayed robot scenario starts at 100,000 units in 2027, grows 50 per cent annually through 2031 and then 25 per cent annually through 2036. The replicated scenario starts at 500,000, doubles annually through 2031 and then grows 50 per cent annually. The accelerated scenario starts at one million, multiplies by 2.5 annually through 2031 and then grows 60 per cent annually. It reaches 62.5 million in 2032, 100 million in 2033 and 409.6 million in 2036.

Annual batch mass equals robots manufactured multiplied by kilograms per robot. The unrounded 2031 accelerated count is 39,062,500. Multiplying by 60 kilograms gives 2,343,750,000 kilograms. Accumulated manufactured mass sums each year's batches. The in-service stock separately deducts observed final retirements.

Market-demand calculations use separate end-use, primary and secondary accounts. Copper uses S and P Global's rounded January 2026 points of 28, 33, 38 and 42 million tonnes in 2025, 2030, 2035 and 2040, with straight interpolation between points. The accelerated robot addition is finished copper in that year's new batch. Factory procurement and primary ore are calculated separately. The main four-sector market total is treated as excluding the separately illustrated humanoid manufacturing scenarios.

Aluminium uses McKinsey's July 2025 demand series of 106 to 130 million tonnes, primary supply of 73 to 78 million and secondary supply of 32 to 48 million from 2025 to 2035. Each series is interpolated at its own compound rate, then extended one year to 2036. This gives demand growth of about 2.06 per cent, primary pipeline growth of 0.665 per cent and secondary pipeline growth of 4.14 per cent annually. The robot addition to end use is finished metal; its factory procurement uses 95 per cent yield. Additional primary and secondary feed is split equally in the half-secondary case. The base-market gap can be filled by either source. Humanoid demand is an additional scenario allowance because the published outlook does not identify its humanoid component; any subsequently identified allowance must be deducted before adding the robot inventory.

All-cause fleet sensitivities apply an annual final-retirement fraction to each cohort: surviving units equal that year's new units multiplied by one minus the fraction, raised to the number of years before 2036. New end-year batches have zero earlier exposure. Accident reductions of 90 and 99 per cent are independent illustrative inputs and are not calibrated to injury rates. Longer-life design hypotheses do not impose a calendar disposal date or automatically release useful equipment as scrap.

The Australian population comparison uses rounded estimates of 27.6 million in June 2025 and 31.5 million in June 2036. Additional dwellings equal additional people divided by 2.5 residents. The civic service requirement is 0.1 robot per resident. Manufacture starts at 5,000 robots in 2027, multiplying by 2.5 annually through 2031 and by 1.6 thereafter. Each year, supported output is the smaller of the manufacturing ambition and commissioned factory capacity. Exports take 35 per cent, while the domestic share enters repair-preserving surviving cohorts. The separate one per cent copper export target applies only to added global copper end use between 2025 and 2036.

Australian module output equals productive floor area multiplied by the Tesla target-to-reported-area ratio, the chosen Australian productivity factor and achieved output fraction. A module's capital includes its building, services, equipment, land, site works, design fees and contingency. New modules enter the construction account in their commissioning year. Shared engineering is added once. Working capital equals current annual output multiplied by variable unit cost and financed months divided by twelve. It remains a separate current cash requirement. Sales and production spending are calculated independently. All financial figures use constant late-2025 Australian dollars; the editable real factory cost multiplier is one in the worked case.

Annual finished-material demand equals new units multiplied by material per unit, plus new material supplied for the scheduled service jobs. External factory feed equals finished material divided by net manufacturing yield. New primary material equals external feed multiplied by the share supplied from primary sources. Factory scrap already recirculated within net yield is counted once.

Primary ore equals primary material divided by ore grade, chemical conversion where needed and recovery to the specified product. Copper uses 0.005 grade and 0.85 recovery. The bauxite example uses four tonnes per tonne of primary aluminium. Rare earth ore uses 0.05 total oxide grade, a 0.20 NdPr oxide share, 0.857 metal content and 0.75 recovery. Each is a reference process requiring project-specific validation.

The three ground-computing energy rates are the compound rate connecting 485 to 950 terawatt-hours over five years, 20 per cent and 30 per cent annually. Each is applied from 2025 through 2036. Rated computing gigawatts equal annual terawatt-hours divided by 8.76 hours-to-energy conversion, 1.2 facility overhead and 0.8 average computing load. Annual net addition is the change in rated capacity between years.

Reference rack mass uses 3,245 pounds converted at 0.45359237 kilograms per pound and a 120 kilowatt rack rating. Rack-base mass multiplies rated capacity by this mass per gigawatt. Annual construction mass multiplies the net capacity increase by the same factor. Actual upgrades and replacement components are added from their specified service programme.

The orbital annual run-rate inputs are zero at the end of 2026, one gigawatt at the end of 2027, ten in 2028, 100 in 2029, 200 in 2030 and 300 from 2031 onwards. Actual commissioning within each year uses the mean of its starting and ending run rates. Commissioned gigawatts multiplied by 10,000 tonnes per gigawatt gives payload manufactured. Accumulated commissioning sums annual completed quantities.

Solar annual energy equals rated capacity multiplied by capacity factor and hours in the year. Silicon mass follows cell area, thickness and density. The orbital example uses 1,363.4 watts per square metre of incident sunlight; the ground panel example uses a 1,000 watt-per-square-metre rating condition. Radiator area follows rejected heat, emissivity and the Stefan-Boltzmann relation at 320 kelvin. Power-beaming source output equals delivered electricity divided by end-to-end efficiency.

Train equivalents divide mass by the reported 28,000 tonne Pilbara payload. The defined swimming-pool space is 50 times 25 times two metres, or 2,500 cubic metres. Solid-metal volume divides tonnes by density in tonnes per cubic metre. Country-electricity comparisons divide annual energy by Australia's 286.8 terawatt-hours in 2025.

Robot, ground-computing, orbital and power-supply modules can be combined only after their project boundaries are matched. Electricity used in orbit is supplied in orbit. A generation plant serves its allocated loads once. The power-system examples size selected designs; the final combined mining requirement needs complete project inventories for structures, networks, chip factories, processing plants and service parts as well as the quantified materials here.
